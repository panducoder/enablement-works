Most enablement roadmaps are lists of good ideas with dates attached. They fail in the same way: the ideas outnumber the hours, the follow-up is cut first, and by the third quarter the plan is a record of what slipped. This guide builds the roadmap the other way round, starting from capacity and three agreed behaviors.

## What is a sales enablement roadmap?

A sales enablement roadmap is a 12-month plan that sets which programs enablement will run, in which order, for whom, with how many hours, and how each will be measured. It is agreed with the CRO and reviewed every quarter.

It is not a content calendar. A content calendar lists assets. A roadmap lists programs, and a program includes the asset plus the launch, the practice, the manager reinforcement and the measurement.

## Step 1: How do you diagnose enablement capacity?

Start by comparing the hours your sales plan needs from enablement with the hours your team has. This one comparison decides everything that follows.

A good diagnosis covers five things:

1. **Capacity.** List next year's hires, launches, kickoff and programs. Estimate hours for each and compare with your team's productive hours (about 1,400 per person a year is our planning assumption). The [enablement capacity calculator](/tools/enablement-capacity-calculator/) does this for you.
2. **Tools.** Calculate the [cost per active user](/guides/cost-per-active-user/) of each enablement tool. Half-used tools often signal missing operations hours, not a bad tool.
3. **Content.** Find your 20 most-used assets and check their age and accuracy.
4. **Behavior.** Listen to calls, or read roleplay scores, and note which behaviors separate top reps from the rest.
5. **Stakeholders.** Interview the CRO, five to ten managers and a few top reps about what they need.

If the gap between hours needed and hours available is large, the roadmap must cut, defer or hand off work. Pretending otherwise produces a plan that fails by spring. The [team size guide](/guides/sales-enablement-team-size/) explains the 1:25–50 rule of thumb behind this check.

## Step 2: What work is already fixed?

Some enablement work is not optional, because the sales plan already commits you to it. Place it on the roadmap first.

- **Onboarding cohorts.** Every planned hire needs a ramp program. The Bridge Group's 2026 AE report, covering 158 B2B companies, found average AE ramp time of 6.2 months, the highest in the study's history. Each cohort needs support for most of that time.
- **Product launches.** Their dates come from product and marketing.
- **The sales kickoff and its 90 days.** The event is the easy part. The certification, roleplays, manager lists and day-30, 60 and 90 reports are where the money pays off. See [the 90-day plan after a kickoff](/guides/sales-kickoff-follow-up/).
- **Compliance and annual certifications,** where your industry requires them.

Then set aside a reserve. Gartner's 2026 survey of 227 chief sales officers found that sales organizations completed an average of four transformations in the previous 12 months. Something will change mid-year. Our rule of thumb is to hold about 15% of hours in reserve for it.

## Step 3: How do you choose three behaviors with the CRO?

Agree three seller behaviors that matter most this year, each observable on calls and tied to a sales stage metric. Three is enough. More than three spreads practice and manager attention too thin to change anything.

Use evidence, not opinion: call reviews, win-loss notes, stage conversion data and what top reps do differently. A good behavior passes four tests:

1. **Observable.** A manager can see or hear it on a call or in a roleplay.
2. **Scorable.** It fits on a short scorecard with clear criteria.
3. **Linked.** It connects to one stage metric.
4. **Coachable.** A manager can coach it in a weekly one-to-one.

| Behavior | Seen on calls as | Linked metric |
|---|---|---|
| Quantify the buyer's problem | Rep records a cost or metric of the problem by the second call | Stage 1 to stage 2 conversion |
| Reach the buying group | Three or more engaged contacts before a proposal | Win rate on proposals |
| Hold price with value | Rep ties price to the quantified problem before discussing discount | Average discount |

Write the three behaviors down and get the CRO to sign them. This matters more than it sounds. The Sales Enablement Collective's 2025 landscape report found that only 43.8% of respondents are aligned with their senior leadership on metrics. A signed list of three behaviors and their metrics closes most of that gap.

## Step 4: How do you sequence programs across the year?

Sequence programs so the fixed work lands first, one behavior is trained at a time, and every program includes its own reinforcement. Six rules keep the sequence realistic:

1. **Fixed work first.** Onboarding, launches and the kickoff go on the calendar before anything else.
2. **One behavior per [skill sprint](/glossary/#skill-sprint).** Train one behavior for about six weeks, with scored practice and manager coaching, then move to the next.
3. **Reinforcement is part of the program.** Memory research has shown for over a century that new material fades fast without review. Murre and Dros's 2015 replication of Ebbinghaus's forgetting curve, published in PLOS ONE, reproduced the classic shape: steep loss soon after learning, then a slower decline. So no program launches without practice, a manager ask and checks at day 30, 60 and 90.
4. **Cap work in progress.** For a team of three, run no more than three major programs at once.
5. **Stagger starts where you can.** Start one team 30 days before another, so you can compare them.
6. **Protect the reserve.** Do not spend it in the first quarter.

## Step 5: How do you set baselines and measures?

Set the baseline for each program before it starts, using four levels of evidence: activity, practice, behavior and outcome. A baseline set after a program cannot show change.

For each program, record the current completion norm, the current practice score, how often the behavior appears on calls today (or in roleplays and manager observations, where calls are not recorded), and the linked stage metric for the last two quarters. The [sales enablement metrics guide](/guides/sales-enablement-metrics/) covers each level.

## Step 6: How do you run the quarterly review?

Hold a 60-minute review with the CRO every quarter, and re-plan the next quarter in detail. A fixed agenda keeps it short:

1. What shipped against plan, and what slipped.
2. Results by program, at each of the four levels.
3. Hours used against plan, and the reserve remaining.
4. Tool usage and cost per active user.
5. What to stop.
6. Next quarter's programs and any change to the three behaviors.

Invite product marketing and customer success leaders. Gartner's 2026 CSO survey found that sales organizations that create enablement content together with marketing and service are 2.4 times more likely to achieve strong commercial growth.

## Example: a roadmap for a 250-rep team

Example: a company has 250 reps and 25 managers. The plan includes 70 new hires, three launches, a January kickoff and three skill sprints. The enablement team has three people, or 4,200 hours.

Using our planning assumptions, the plan needs 8,200 hours: onboarding 2,100, launches 900, kickoff and follow-up 1,000, skill sprints 900, coaching support 1,800, and content, tools and reporting 1,500. A 15% reserve adds about 1,200, for 9,400 in total. The gap is 5,200 hours.

The CRO makes four decisions. Drop the third skill sprint (saves 300 hours). Hire one person in the second quarter (adds about 1,000 hours this year). Hand off production work worth 3,500 hours. Reduce the reserve to 800 hours. Need and capacity now both come to 8,700 hours.

| Quarter | Programs | Measures |
|---|---|---|
| Q1 | Kickoff plus 90-day follow-up; onboarding rebuild; baselines for behaviors 1 and 2 | Certification rate by week 2; day-30, 60 and 90 reports |
| Q2 | Skill sprint 1: quantify the problem; launch A; new hire joins | Practice scores; behavior on calls; stage 1 to 2 conversion |
| Q3 | Skill sprint 2: reach the buying group; launch B; tool review | Engaged contacts per proposal; cost per active user |
| Q4 | Launch C; next year's capacity model and kickoff design | Win rate on proposals; ramp time for Q2 cohort |

## What to do this quarter

1. Run the capacity comparison: hours needed against hours available.
2. Place onboarding, launches and the kickoff on a 12-month calendar.
3. Draft three behaviors with evidence, and get the CRO to sign them.
4. Decide with the CRO how to close the hours gap: cut, defer, hire or hand off.
5. Set baselines for the first two programs.
6. Book the four quarterly reviews now.
