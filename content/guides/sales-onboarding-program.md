Most sales onboarding programs start on day one with a laptop, a login list and a binder. The rep fills in forms about accounts the company already knows, and the manager is asked to build a plan from scratch in a busy week. The result is a slow first month and a long ramp. This guide shows what to set up before day one, gives a 30/60/90 ramp plan, and defines the metrics that show whether onboarding works.

## Why does sales onboarding take so long?

Sales ramp is getting longer, mostly because deals are more complex. The Bridge Group's 2026 report on account executive models, based on 158 B2B companies, put average ramp at 6.2 months, the highest in the study's history. The report links the rise to deal complexity and larger buying committees, especially at higher deal sizes, and notes that companies are asking for more prior experience as ramp costs rise.

Onboarding quality is also low in general. Gallup found that only 12% of US employees say their company does a good job of onboarding, and only 29% of new hires feel fully prepared and supported to excel after onboarding. The same Gallup research found that employees were 3.4 times as likely to feel their onboarding was successful when their manager took an active role.

Both findings point to one lever: the manager, equipped early. See the [glossary entry for ramp time](/glossary/#ramp-time).

## What should be set up before a new sales rep's first day?

Everything the company already knows should be ready before the rep arrives. Onboarding still asks the rep for data the company holds. Read what exists instead, draft a plan, and let the manager edit it.

**Pre-start checklist (complete by day -3):**

1. **Access.** CRM, email, calendar, call recording, content platform, roleplay tool and LMS accounts work, and the rep is in the right Slack or Teams channels.
2. **Territory.** The account list, open opportunities, stages and last touches are pulled from the CRM into one view.
3. **The rep's baseline.** Prior industry, prior roles and a scheduled baseline skill assessment in week one.
4. **A draft 30-day plan.** Which accounts to research first, which skill to practice first, which calls to shadow.
5. **Roleplays from real accounts.** Two or three practice scenarios built from the rep's own territory, not generic personas.
6. **A buddy.** A named peer for daily questions in the first two weeks.
7. **Manager time.** Thirty minutes a day blocked in week one, and two coaching slots a week for the first month.

The manager's job is to confirm, not build. Ten minutes to review and edit a draft plan is realistic; building one from a blank page gets postponed. AI tools can draft much of this from CRM data. The limit: a messy CRM produces a confident, wrong plan, so the manager's review matters.

## The 30/60/90-day sales onboarding ramp plan

The plan moves the rep from learning to supervised selling to owning a pipeline. Adjust the milestones to your sales cycle; these fit a mid-market account executive with a cycle of about 60 to 90 days.

| Phase | Focus | Milestones | Evidence | Manager's job |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Days -14 to 0 | Pre-start | Checklist complete, draft plan approved | Plan confirmed by manager | Review and edit the draft plan |
| Days 1–30 | Learn and certify | Buyer problems, product and sales motion learned. 10 calls shadowed. 5 scored roleplays from own accounts. Certification passed at 70 or above. First meetings booked. | Certification score, roleplay scores, meetings booked | Daily check-in in week one, two coaching sessions a week |
| Days 31–60 | Supervised selling | Rep leads discovery calls with manager present. First opportunities created. Pipeline built to an agreed target. | Calls led, manager call notes, opportunities created | Joins or reviews two calls a week, coaches one behavior |
| Days 61–90 | Own the pipeline | Rep runs full cycles alone. Deals advance past discovery. First win or a late-stage opportunity. | Stage progression, first deal or late-stage deal | One live-deal coaching session a week |
| Day 91 to full ramp | Build to quota | Monthly attainment climbs toward full quota | Attainment by month against the ramp curve | Normal coaching rhythm, tracked in coaching coverage |

### What to certify in the first 30 days

Test situations, not knowledge. A quiz about product features belongs in the LMS. The certification should ask the rep to handle a realistic moment with a scored buyer. One moment matters most for new reps: the opening of the second call. New reps often run good discovery, then open call two with a product demo, and everything learned in call one disappears. Certify that they can start call two by saying the buyer's problem back, asking what they got wrong and putting a number on it. See [problem-first selling](/guides/problem-first-selling/).

## Which sales onboarding metrics should you track?

Track the time it takes a rep to do real selling things, not how many modules they complete.

| Metric | Formula | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Time to first qualified meeting | Days from start date to the first meeting that meets your qualification rule | The earliest sign the rep can open conversations |
| Time to first opportunity | Days from start date to first opportunity created and accepted | Shows pipeline building has begun |
| Time to first deal | Days from start date to close of the first won deal the rep owned | Shows the rep can run a full cycle |
| Ramp time | Months from start date to the first month at or above full quota | The headline measure of onboarding |
| Certification score | Score on the 30-day certification, 0 to 100 | Early predictor to compare cohorts |

Report these by hiring cohort. A cohort that starts in January and one that starts in June face different pipelines, so compare each against its own baseline. See the [glossary entry for time to first deal](/glossary/#time-to-first-deal).

## Where do new reps learn judgment now?

New reps used to learn judgment through volume. Illustrative numbers for one junior rep's week: 200 dials built pattern recognition, 40 real conversations brought first objections, 10 genuine pushbacks gave practice in judgment, 2 recoveries were worth coaching and 1 lesson stuck. Now AI handles more of the dials and first conversations, and junior roles are scarcer.

The broader labor data shows the same direction. Erik Brynjolfsson, Bharat Chandar and Ruyu Chen at the Stanford Digital Economy Lab report that employment of 22–25-year-olds in AI-exposed occupations now stands 19% below where it would be had it kept pace with less-exposed peers (August 2026 revision), mainly through reduced hiring. The paper does not single out sales, so treat it as context, not a sales statistic.

The implication for onboarding: if real situations are scarcer, simulated situations with a score are how you refill the practice on purpose. Scored [AI roleplay](/guides/ai-roleplay-sales-training/) built from the rep's own accounts is the most direct way. The limit: a simulated objection is not a lost deal. The sting that makes a lesson stick is weaker. Simulation shortens the path; it does not replace real calls.

## Example: what one month of faster ramp is worth

Example: a company hires 40 account executives a year. Each carries an annual quota of $1.2 million, or $100,000 a month at full ramp. Average ramp is 6 months.

If a pre-start checklist and a 30/60/90 plan cut ramp by one month, each new rep reaches full productivity a month earlier. That adds about $100,000 of quota capacity per rep, or 40 × $100,000 = $4 million of capacity a year. Capacity is not revenue: apply your win rate and attainment to estimate the revenue effect. If the team attains 70% of quota on average, that is roughly $2.8 million of additional bookings potential, before any change in retention.

The cost side is mostly hours: about 6 enablement hours per new hire to prepare the pre-start pack, and about 8 manager hours in the first month. For 40 hires, that is 240 enablement hours a year.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting on day one.** Access problems and blank plans waste the first week.
- **Measuring completion.** Finishing 40 modules says nothing about whether the rep can run call two.
- **Generic roleplays.** Practice built from fictional companies feels like school. Build it from the rep's own accounts.
- **Leaving the manager out.** Gallup's 3.4 times finding is about the manager, not the content.
- **One ramp target for every role.** SDRs, mid-market AEs and enterprise AEs ramp at different speeds. Set targets by role.

## What are the limits of this ramp plan?

A ramp plan cannot fix a bad territory, an unclear ideal customer profile or a broken lead flow. It also depends on CRM data quality for the pre-start pack. Ramp benchmarks vary widely by deal size and segment, so treat the Bridge Group's 6.2 months as context and set your own baseline by role.
