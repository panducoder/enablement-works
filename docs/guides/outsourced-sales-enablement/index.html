Outsourced sales enablement: what to keep in-house and what to hand off

Capacity and ROIUpdated 6 November 20267 min readBy the Enablement Works editorial team

Short answer

Keep strategy in-house: choosing the behaviors that matter, setting priorities with the CRO, approving content and presenting results. Hand off production and operations: building assets, loading them into your tools, launching programs, chasing completion and assembling reports. A good partner works in your tools and methodology, under your approval, with a defined monthly production volume.

Key takeaways

  • Strategy is the scarce part of enablement; production and operations can be done by others to the enablement leader's standard.
  • Keep the decide, approve and present work in-house; hand off the build, load, launch, chase and compile work.
  • Marketing already works this way: an ANA survey found 82% of members run an in-house agency and 92% also use external agencies.
  • Judge a partner on whether it works in your tools and methodology, reviews quality before you see it, and measures beyond completion.
  • A subscription with a fixed production menu and a cap on work in progress keeps scope and cost predictable.

Outsourcing sales enablement does not mean handing your sales strategy to an outside firm. Done well, it means the reverse: your enablement leader keeps the judgment work and stops spending it on production. This guide sets out what to keep, what to hand off, how to choose a partner, which contracts work and what can go wrong.

What is outsourced sales enablement?

Outsourced sales enablement is the use of an outside firm to do part of the enablement function's work, usually production and program operations, while the internal team keeps strategy and approval. It sits between hiring and doing nothing.

There are five common forms:

  • Sales training firms bring their own methodology and deliver workshops.
  • Content agencies design decks, videos and battlecards to a brief.
  • Freelancers and contractors add hours for a specific project.
  • Fractional enablement leaders provide part-time strategy.
  • Production and operations partners build, launch and run programs in your tools, under your strategy.

This guide focuses on the last form, because it addresses the most common gap: the hours between a good plan and a finished, adopted program.

Why are enablement teams handing off work?

Enablement teams hand off work because strategy is the scarce part of enablement and most of their hours go elsewhere.

Ask a head of enablement what they would do with ten more hours a week. The answers are almost always strategic: sit with front-line managers, listen to calls, rebuild onboarding around what top reps do, agree with the CRO which three behaviors matter this year. Then look at where their hours go: formatting decks, recording videos, loading modules, chasing completion, pulling reports by hand.

Headcount is not keeping up. The Sales Enablement Collective's 2025 landscape report found that 23.6% of respondents' teams shrank in headcount over the previous 12 months. Across business functions, the reasons for outsourcing have also shifted. Deloitte's 2024 Global Outsourcing Survey of more than 500 executives found that skilled talent and agility have joined cost reduction as key drivers.

Marketing solved this decades ago. A marketing leader sets strategy, and agencies and production teams build much of the output. Nobody thinks the marketing leader matters less for it. The ANA's 2023 survey of 162 marketers found that 82% of members have an in-house agency and 92% of respondents also work with external agencies. Among those using outside agencies, the average share of work done in-house is 61%. The mix is normal. The question is which work goes where.

What should you keep in-house and what should you hand off?

Keep the work that needs your company's judgment and authority. Hand off the work that needs skill and hours but can be done to your standard by someone else.

Work Keep in-house Hand off Why
Enablement strategy and charter Yes Sets what enablement is for and what it will not do
Choosing three priority behaviors with the CRO Yes Needs authority and access to sales leadership
Approving briefs, claims and final assets Yes Accuracy and compliance are your accountability
Relationships with front-line managers Yes Reinforcement depends on trust
Presenting results to the CRO Yes The enablement leader should own the story
Writing, designing and recording assets Yes Skilled, but repeatable to a standard
Building modules, roleplays and certifications in your tools Yes Hours-heavy and tool-specific
Launching programs, nudging reps, manager lists Yes Routine but time-critical
Platform administration Yes Rarely the best use of a strategist
Pulling data and drafting the monthly report Yes You review and present it
Live facilitation and kickoff production Either Either Depends on your team's strengths

The rule in short: decide, approve and present stay in-house; build, load, launch, chase and compile can go.

How do you evaluate an outsourced enablement partner?

Evaluate a partner on how it fits your methodology, tools and approval process, and on what it measures. A polished portfolio matters less than these ten checks.

  1. Works in your tools. It builds in your LMS, content platform and roleplay tool, with no proprietary platform you must adopt.
  2. Learns your methodology. It keeps your methodology, personas, approved claims and scorecard in one reference kit and checks every asset against it.
  3. Reviews quality before you see it. A senior reviewer approves every asset before it reaches your approver.
  4. Uses a fixed approval process. A named approver on your side, with a set number of consolidated review rounds.
  5. Measures beyond completion. It reports practice, behavior and outcomes, not only activity. See the four levels of evidence.
  6. Defines volume clearly. You know what a month buys and how many briefs can be in production at once.
  7. Stays tool-neutral. It does not resell licenses or take vendor referral fees, so its tool advice is not paid for.
  8. Handles data and AI responsibly. Enterprise AI agreements, no training on your data, a data protection agreement and a sub-processor list.
  9. Gives you ownership. You own all content and data and can export it at any time.
  10. Has an exit plan. You can see how work transfers back to your team or another partner.

Ask for sample assets built for a comparable sales motion, and a sample monthly report with names removed.

Which contract models are common?

Five contract models are common. The right one depends on whether your need is a single project or a continuous flow of programs.

Model How it works Best for Watch out for
Fixed-fee project One price for a defined deliverable An onboarding rebuild, a kickoff Change requests and no follow-up after delivery
Hourly or day rate Pay for time used Short, uncertain work Cost is hard to predict; no incentive to be efficient
Retainer Monthly fee for a block of hours Steady, varied requests Unused hours expire; output is not defined
Subscription with production menu Monthly fee for a defined volume of assets and a cap on work in progress Continuous program flow Needs a clear menu and a visible usage log
Outcome-linked fee Part of the fee depends on agreed results Mature measurement Disputes over attribution

As one example of the subscription model, Enablement Works starts with a three-week Diagnostic and then runs a 12-month subscription with a fixed production menu, a cap on briefs in production at once, and a monthly report to the CRO.

Example: what changes when production moves out

Example: a company with 400 reps has a three-person enablement team, or about 4,200 productive hours a year. Our 1:25–50 rule of thumb puts the plan's need at 8 to 16 people. Check yours with the enablement capacity calculator.

Before the hand-off, the team spends about 3,000 hours on production and operations and 1,200 hours on strategy: manager work, call listening, program design and CRO reviews.

After handing off most production and operations, the team keeps about 600 hours for approvals and partner reviews. Strategy time rises from 1,200 to about 3,600 hours. The partner supplies the production hours, and the total capacity now matches more of the plan.

The point is not the total. It is that the most expensive judgment in the team is now spent on judgment.

What are the risks, and how do you reduce them?

The main risks are generic content, loss of internal knowledge and unclear scope. Each has a specific control.

Risk Control
Generic, off-message content A shared reference kit, your approval on every asset, two consolidated review rounds
Knowledge leaves with the partner You keep strategy and briefs; all assets live in your tools; full export rights
Scope creep and surprise cost A production menu, a work-in-progress cap and a visible usage log
Managers see it as an outside program Launch under your sales leader's name and your brand
Tool bias A partner that does not resell or take referral fees
Data exposure A data protection agreement, zero data retention for AI, coaching scores not used in performance reviews

When should you not outsource sales enablement?

Do not outsource when nobody inside owns strategy, when your methodology is not yet defined, or when the team is small enough that one person can cover the work. In our experience, under about 50 reps a single enablement hire plus product marketing is usually enough. And if your content needs regulatory approval, set up the approval workflow before adding an outside producer.

Common mistakes

  • Outsourcing strategy instead of production. A partner cannot choose your priority behaviors for you.
  • Buying hours instead of outputs. Define what a month delivers, not only what it costs.
  • Skipping the reference kit. Without it, every asset becomes a rewrite.
  • Measuring the partner on volume. Measure on programs completed and behaviors seen on calls.
  • Adding a new platform with the partner. Use the tools you already pay for first.

Frequently asked questions

Is outsourcing sales enablement the same as hiring a sales training company?

No. A sales training company usually brings its own methodology and delivers workshops. An outsourced enablement partner works in your methodology and your tools, building and running programs your team designs. Some companies use both: a training firm for the methodology and a production partner to embed it.

How much does outsourced sales enablement cost?

Prices vary widely by model. Fixed-fee projects are priced per deliverable, such as an onboarding program. Retainers and subscriptions are priced per month for a defined production volume. Compare options on cost per adopted output, such as cost per program completed on time, rather than on monthly fee alone.

Can a company with no enablement team outsource enablement?

It can outsource production, but someone inside must still own strategy: agreeing priorities with the CRO, approving content and acting on results. Without an internal owner, a partner ends up guessing priorities. In companies without an enablement hire, a sales leader or RevOps lead often takes this owner role.

How long does it take an outsourced enablement partner to ramp up?

Expect two to six weeks before the first assets ship. The partner needs your methodology, personas, approved claims, brand rules and tool access. A short diagnostic at the start speeds this up, because it produces the client kit and the first program briefs together.

Will reps know the content was built by an outside team?

They should not need to. Programs launch under your sales leader's name, in your tools, in your brand and methodology. What reps notice is whether the content is accurate and relevant, which depends on your approval process more than on who built it.

What is a fractional head of enablement?

A fractional head of enablement is an experienced enablement leader who works part-time for several companies, usually setting strategy and building the first programs. It suits companies too small for a full-time leader. It is the opposite of a production partner: it outsources strategy rather than production.
