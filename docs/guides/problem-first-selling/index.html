Problem-first selling: what buyers still need from sellers when AI handles the information

Buyer-first sellingUpdated 6 November 20266 min readBy the Enablement Works editorial team

Short answer

Problem-first selling means opening with the buyer's problem, in their words and with their number, before showing your product. When AI handles product information, buyers still need sellers for six jobs: name the problem, put a cost on it, bring one insight they lacked, build consensus, de-risk the plan, and help them justify it internally.

Key takeaways

  • Gartner found 61% of B2B buyers prefer a rep-free buying experience, but buyers still seek seller input on whether a solution fits their situation.
  • When AI covers the product facts, sellers earn their place through six jobs, all about the buyer's problem.
  • The pivot from discovery to the problem statement has a fixed place: the first five minutes of call two.
  • Gartner found 73% of B2B buyers actively avoid suppliers who send irrelevant outreach; default AI-written email is a fast way to become irrelevant.
  • Build lookalike target lists from why customers changed, not from their firmographic profile.

A buyer can now ask an AI assistant to compare five vendors, summarize their reviews and draft questions for the demo. The product facts that sellers used to carry are a prompt away. So what do buyers still need from a seller? The answer is help with their problem, not more about your catalog. This guide sets out the six jobs that remain, shows what changes in a first meeting, and gives a worked example.

What do buyers still need from sellers when AI handles the information?

Buyers still need sellers for judgment about their own situation: what the problem is, what it costs, and whether a solution fits. A Gartner survey of 632 B2B buyers, published in June 2025, found 61% prefer a rep-free buying experience overall. The same research found buyers prefer seller input for tasks that need contextual judgment, such as whether a product fits their company's needs. A Gartner analyst advised that sellers should offer unique guidance and act as a sounding board, rather than repeat generic information.

Buyers also arrive later and better informed. 6sense's 2025 Buyer Experience Report, based on nearly 4,000 buyers, found first contact came when buyers were about 61% through their buying process. Buyers initiated that contact 79% of the time. They bought from the vendor they ranked first 77% of the time.

Put together: the information job has moved to the buyer's own research and their AI. The decision job is still open, and that is where a seller earns a place.

What are the six jobs of a problem-first seller?

A problem-first seller does six jobs, and every one is about the buyer's problem:

Job What it sounds like What it replaces
1. Name the problem "Your stage-2 drop-off", in the buyer's words "Pipeline challenges"
2. Put a cost on it Money per quarter, from the buyer's numbers Generic ROI claims
3. Bring one insight One number or pattern they had not considered A 40-slide deck
4. Build consensus A problem statement the whole group agrees with Winning only the champion
5. De-risk it What happens if the plan is wrong, and the checkpoint "Trust us"
6. Help them justify it A one-page case they can forward without you A PDF proposal by email

The job most teams skip is number 4. Sellers optimize for the champion and forget the other people who can quietly say no. Gartner found buying groups that reach consensus are 2.5 times more likely to report a high-quality deal. See buying groups and consensus.

What changes in a problem-first first meeting?

The first meeting changes from the vendor's world to the buyer's problem. Many first meetings still open with who we are, our logos, our modules and our best case study. The buyer sits through it, waiting for it to become about them. The difference shows in five places:

Moment Vendor-first Problem-first
Slide 1 Your logos Their problem, stated back in their words, with their number
The middle Your feature list What the problem costs per quarter
Proof Your best customer story Evidence from their own funnel, such as where stage-2 deals stall
The demo First Only after you agree the path
The proposal Emailed as a PDF Walked through on a call where you can hear objections

The realism line: you cannot name their number if discovery did not find one. Problem-first selling starts in call one, not on slide one.

Where does the pivot from discovery to the problem happen?

The pivot happens in the first five minutes of call two. A common pattern among newer reps: call one goes well, with good questions and a curious buyer. Then call two opens with "let me show you the platform", and everything learned disappears into a demo. Use a fixed sequence:

  1. Call one is discovery. No pitch. Find the problem and who owns it.
  2. Open call two with it. "Here is what I heard last time. Tell me what I got wrong."
  3. Put a number on it. What does this cost per quarter, in their numbers?
  4. Show the path in three steps, not the full catalog.
  5. Confirm the sponsor. Who signs, and do they see the problem the same way?
  6. Only then, propose.

The moment a buyer corrects your problem statement is the most valuable minute in the deal. You learn what they care about, and they start to own the story. Some buyers want the demo first. Give it, then still state the problem back before you discuss price.

How should AI-written follow-up change?

AI-written follow-up should carry the buyer's words and the next decision, not a default template. Gartner found 73% of B2B buyers actively avoid suppliers who send irrelevant outreach. If every seller uses the same assistant with the same default prompt, every email reads the same: a polite opening, a paragraph about the vendor, a soft ask. The buyer's own AI may summarize it as "vendor follow-up, no action needed".

Emails that get replies differ in five ways:

  • The opening repeats something the buyer said, not a pleasantry.
  • The ask is specific: two time slots, not "would be great to connect".
  • There is one decision to make, not zero and not three.
  • It is about five lines, not nine.
  • It sounds like the conversation you actually had.

AI drafts are fine. The fix is the input: give the model the buyer's words, their number and the next decision. A great email cannot save a weak call. It can only carry the problem forward.

How should targeting change?

Targeting should start from why customers changed, not from what they look like. AI agents can now take one good customer and find 20 lookalikes in minutes. But most lookalike methods copy the profile, and buyers do not buy on profile:

Lookalike seed What it copies What it misses
Firmographics Size, industry, region Whether they feel the problem
Titles Who signs Who lives with the pain every day
Behavior signals What they post, which tools they use Why the last customer changed
Intent signals Site visits, hiring Whether the cost of the problem is visible yet
Problem statement from the win The named cost of the status quo Little; this is what predicts a purchase

Start the search from "why they changed", then filter on firmographics. At the top of the funnel, profile lookalikes still beat nothing. But they fill a list, not a pipeline. If your CRM has no field for why the customer changed, add one.

Example: a problem statement with a number

Example: in call one, a seller learns that the buyer's team creates 400 stage-2 opportunities a quarter. Conversion from stage 2 to stage 3 has fallen from 40% to 30%. Average deal size is $25,000, and about a quarter of stage-3 deals close.

The seller opens call two like this: "Last time you said stage-2 deals stall after the first demo. Conversion dropped from 40% to 30%. That is 40 fewer stage-3 deals a quarter. At your close rate and deal size, about $250,000 a quarter in bookings. Did I get that right?"

The buyer corrects one number: the close rate is closer to 20%. The cost becomes $200,000 a quarter. That correction is the point. The buyer now owns the number, and it goes on the one-page case the champion forwards to finance.

How do you train problem-first selling?

Train it as a skill with practice, scoring and manager follow-up, not as a workshop. Three parts work well:

  • A roleplay of the call-two opening, practiced weekly for six weeks. See AI roleplay for sales training.
  • Three scored behaviors: states the problem back, quantifies the cost, confirms who else must agree. Use one talk track and one skills scorecard.
  • One manager question in each one-to-one: "What number did the buyer give you, and did they correct it?"

Start in onboarding, where habits form fastest.

Common mistakes

  • Opening with the company story. The buyer has read it, or asked their AI.
  • Running discovery and never saying the problem out loud. The pivot belongs in the first five minutes of call two.
  • Using the seller's numbers instead of the buyer's. A cost the buyer did not supply is a claim, not a problem statement.
  • Bringing ten insights. Bring one. A number, not a deck.
  • Sending default AI-written follow-ups. Feed the model the buyer's words and the next decision.

Frequently asked questions

What is problem-first selling?

Problem-first selling is a sales approach that starts with the buyer's problem, stated in the buyer's words and costed with the buyer's numbers, before any product is shown. The product enters only once the buyer agrees the problem is real and worth solving.

Do B2B buyers still want to talk to sales reps?

Less than before, and for different reasons. A Gartner survey published in June 2025 found 61% of B2B buyers prefer a rep-free experience overall. But buyers still prefer seller input for judgment tasks, such as deciding whether a product fits their company's needs. That is where sellers now add value.

How is AI changing the B2B buying process?

Buyers use AI assistants to compare vendors, summarize reviews and prepare questions before they talk to anyone. 6sense's 2025 research found buyers were about 61% through their buying process at first contact with a seller. Sellers now meet buyers who know the facts and need help with the decision.

How do you transition from discovery to the demo?

Do not go straight from discovery to the demo. Open the next call by stating the problem back and asking the buyer what you got wrong, then put a cost on it and show a three-step path. Demo only after the buyer agrees on the problem and the path.

What if the buyer asks for a demo first?

Give it. Some buyers want to see the product before they discuss problems. Keep it short, then still state the problem back and agree its cost before you discuss price.

How should sellers use AI to write emails?

Use AI for drafts, but give it what only you know: the buyer's exact words, their number and the next decision. Default prompts produce emails that read like every other vendor's, which the buyer's own AI may summarize as 'no action needed'.
