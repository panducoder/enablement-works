# How many sales enablement people do you need? The 1:25–50 rule of thumb

> Our rule of thumb is one sales enablement person for every 25 to 50 quota-carrying reps. Divide your rep count by 25 and by 50 to get the range. Move toward 1:25 when you hire many reps, launch often or expect enablement to support coaching. Move toward 1:50 when the sales motion is stable and managers coach on their own.

Source: https://enablement.works/guides/sales-enablement-team-size/ · Updated 2026-11-06 · Enablement Works

## Key takeaways

- Our rule of thumb: one enablement person supports 25 to 50 quota-carrying reps, depending on onboarding, launch and coaching load.
- Divide your rep count by 25 and by 50; that range is the enablement team your sales plan quietly assumes.
- An hours model is more precise than a ratio: list the work the plan needs, estimate hours per item and divide by about 1,400 productive hours per person.
- A capacity gap does not disappear; it shows up as kickoffs with no follow-up, late launches and new hires who ramp by shadowing.
- No verified public benchmark for the enablement-to-rep ratio exists, so treat any single number, including ours, as a starting point.

Most sales plans size the work for enablement in detail: new hires, launches, a kickoff, certifications. Few size the hours. This guide gives you a quick ratio, a more precise hours model and a worked example, so you can put both numbers on one page before the plan is final.

## What is the rule of thumb for sales enablement team size?

Our rule of thumb is one sales enablement person for every 25 to 50 quota-carrying reps. The low end (1:25) fits teams with heavy onboarding, many launches or enablement-led coaching. The high end (1:50) fits stable teams with experienced managers.

We say "rule of thumb" on purpose. We looked for a public, primary benchmark on the [enablement-to-rep ratio](/glossary/#enablement-to-rep-ratio) and did not find one we could verify. Several vendor blogs quote ratios in the same range, but none publishes a method or sample. Treat any single ratio, including ours, as a starting point to test against your own hours.

What the public data does show is pressure on headcount. The Sales Enablement Collective's 2025 landscape report found that 23.6% of respondents' teams shrank over the previous 12 months. Sales plans rarely shrank by the same amount.

## How do you do the arithmetic?

Count your quota-carrying reps. Divide by 25, then by 50. That is the range of enablement people your sales plan assumes. Then count the people you actually have.

| Quota-carrying reps | At 1:50 | At 1:25 |
|---|---|---|
| 100 | 2 | 4 |
| 200 | 4 | 8 |
| 300 | 6 | 12 |
| 500 | 10 | 20 |
| 1,000 | 20 | 40 |

If the plan assumes four to eight people and you have two, the gap does not disappear. It is not a judgment of the enablement team. It is a planning error: the work was sized and the hours were not.

You can run your own numbers in the [enablement capacity calculator](/tools/enablement-capacity-calculator/).

## What moves you toward 1:25 and what moves you toward 1:50?

Five drivers decide where you sit in the range. Score each from 0 to 2, then add them up. This is our working heuristic, not a published standard.

| Driver | 0 points | 1 point | 2 points |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hiring volume (new hires per year as a share of reps) | Under 15% | 15–30% | Over 30% |
| Launches and major message changes per year | 0–1 | 2–3 | 4 or more |
| Coaching load | Managers coach on their own | Enablement provides coaching packs | Enablement runs scored roleplays and weekly coaching cards |
| Complexity (segments, product lines, languages) | One motion, one language | Two or three | Four or more |
| Production help (designers, video, LMS admin) | Full in-house or partner support | Partial | None |

**How to read the score:**

- **0–3 points:** plan near 1:50.
- **4–6 points:** plan near 1:35 to 1:40.
- **7–10 points:** plan near 1:25.

Hiring volume carries the most weight in practice, because onboarding work scales with every new hire. The Bridge Group's 2026 AE report, covering 158 B2B companies, found average AE ramp time of 6.2 months, the highest in the study's history. Longer ramps mean each cohort of new hires needs enablement support for longer.

Coaching load is the driver most teams underestimate. Gartner's 2024 survey of 1,026 B2B sellers found that 72% feel overwhelmed by the number of skills their job requires. Teaching a skill once is not enough. Someone has to build the practice, the scorecard and the manager materials that keep it alive.

## How do you size enablement by hours instead of headcount?

An hours model is more precise than a ratio. List the work the sales plan needs, estimate hours for each item, add them up and divide by the productive hours of one enablement person.

These are our planning assumptions. Replace them with your own history where you have it.

| Work item | Planning assumption |
|---|---|
| Productive hours per enablement person per year | 1,400 (after meetings, leave and admin) |
| Onboarding, per new hire | 30 hours (cohort sessions, certification review, updates) |
| Product or message launch | 300 hours each |
| Sales kickoff plus 90-day follow-up | 1,000 hours |
| Skill program or certification | 300 hours each |
| Coaching support | 6 hours per sales manager per month |
| Content upkeep, tool admin and reporting | 6 hours per rep per year |

The kickoff line includes the follow-up on purpose. Many teams budget the event and forget the 90 days after it. See [after the sales kickoff](/guides/sales-kickoff-follow-up/) for what those 90 days need.

## Example: sizing enablement for a 300-rep team

Example: a software company has 300 quota-carrying reps and 30 sales managers. Next year's plan includes 90 new hires, four product launches, one kickoff and three skill programs. The enablement team has three people.

| Work item | Calculation | Hours |
|---|---|---|
| Onboarding | 90 hires × 30 | 2,700 |
| Launches | 4 × 300 | 1,200 |
| Kickoff and 90-day follow-up | 1 × 1,000 | 1,000 |
| Skill programs | 3 × 300 | 900 |
| Coaching support | 30 managers × 6 × 12 months | 2,160 |
| Content, tools and reporting | 300 reps × 6 | 1,800 |
| **Total needed** | | **9,760** |

The plan needs 9,760 hours. Divided by 1,400 hours per person, that is about seven people, or one per 43 reps. That sits inside the 1:25–50 range.

The team has three people, or 4,200 hours. The gap is 5,560 hours, roughly four people.

Now run the same company in a high-growth year: 150 new hires, six launches and four skill programs. The total rises to 12,460 hours, about nine people, or one per 34 reps. The ratio moved toward 1:25 because hiring and launches grew, not because the rep count did.

If your hours model lands outside 1:25–50, trust the hours model. The ratio is a quick check. The hours are the plan.

## Who should be on a sales enablement team?

A sales enablement team needs a mix of strategy, production and operations skills, not five copies of the same role. The ratio tells you how many people; the mix decides what they can deliver.

| Role | Main work | When to add it |
|---|---|---|
| Head of enablement | Priorities with the CRO, program design, results | First hire |
| Program manager | Onboarding, launches, skill programs end to end | From about 75 reps |
| Content and instructional designer | Assets, modules, roleplay scenarios | When launches or onboarding outgrow the leader's time |
| Enablement operations | Platform admin, launches, nudges, reporting | When you run two or more enablement tools |

Many teams of three are three program managers. They spend a large share of their week on production and operations, because nobody else does it. A designer or an operations role, in-house or through a partner, often adds more delivered programs than a fourth program manager would.

## What are your options when the team is too small?

You have five options, and most teams combine two or three.

| Option | What it does | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Cut scope | Agree three priority behaviors with the CRO and drop the rest | Some requests go unmet; you must say no in writing |
| Hire | Adds permanent capacity | Slow to recruit and ramp; fixed cost |
| Borrow manager time | Managers run roleplays and coaching | Competes with selling time; quality varies |
| Buy tools | Automates some delivery | Tools need someone to fill and run them |
| Hand off production and operations | Frees enablement hours for strategy | Needs a clear brief, approval process and partner standards |

Buying a tool is the option most often chosen and least often sufficient. A platform adds a second job: running it. The [outsourced sales enablement guide](/guides/outsourced-sales-enablement/) covers which work you can hand off safely.

## What does the ratio not tell you?

The ratio does not tell you about skill mix, quality or where hours actually go. Three limits are worth stating plainly.

- **Two teams with the same ratio can perform very differently.** A team of five strategists with no production help may deliver less than a team of three with a designer and an LMS administrator.
- **It ignores other audiences.** If enablement also supports SDRs, sales engineers or customer success, count them, weighted by their program load.
- **AI changes the production hours, not the operations hours.** Drafting is faster. Launching, chasing completion, supporting managers and reporting take about as long as before.

## What to do this quarter

1. Count your quota-carrying reps and divide by 25 and by 50.
2. Score the five drivers to see where in the range you belong.
3. List next year's planned hires, launches, kickoff and programs, and estimate hours with the table above.
4. Count your team's productive hours (people × 1,400).
5. Put the hours needed and the hours available on one slide for your CRO, with the three options you recommend to close the gap.

## FAQ

### What is a good sales enablement to rep ratio?

Our rule of thumb is 1:25 to 1:50. Fast-growing teams with heavy onboarding, frequent launches or enablement-led coaching need the richer end. Stable teams with experienced managers can run near 1:50. We have not found a primary, audited benchmark, so validate the ratio with an hours model.

### Do sales engineers and customer success managers count as reps?

Count every role that enablement is expected to train and support. If enablement also onboards sales engineers, SDRs or customer success managers, include them, but weight them by how much program work they need. A team that supports 200 sellers and 100 customer success managers has a larger load than one with 200 sellers alone.

### How many sales reps can one enablement manager support?

One experienced enablement manager can usually support 25 to 50 reps when they own the full cycle of building, launching, reinforcing and reporting programs. With dedicated production help, from a designer, an instructional designer or a partner, the same person can cover more reps because their hours go to strategy and stakeholder work.

### Should the first enablement hire be a strategist or a producer?

Hire for judgment first: someone who can agree priorities with the CRO and design programs. Production can be added later through junior hires, contractors or a partner. A producer without a strategy owner tends to build whatever is requested, which fills the calendar without changing behavior.

### Does AI reduce how many enablement people you need?

AI cuts the time to draft assets, so production hours drop. It does little for operations: launching programs, chasing completion, supporting managers and reporting. In our experience the ratio moves a little toward 1:50, not to 1:100, because the work AI removes was rarely the bottleneck.

### What happens if the enablement team is too small?

The work the plan needs still exists, so it shows up elsewhere. Common signs are a kickoff with no follow-up, launches reps learn from a slide deck, new hires who ramp by shadowing whoever has time, and tools that sit half-used.
